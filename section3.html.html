Chapter 15

Microbial Mechanisms of Pathogenicity

How a pathogen gets in, holds on, gets past your defenses, does damage, and gets out.

15.1 · Key terms

Pathogenicity vs. virulence

Pathogenicity

The ability to cause disease. It is a yes-or-no question: can this microbe make a host sick at all?

Virulence

The degree of pathogenicity. How easily, and how badly, does this microbe cause disease?

Think of pathogenicity as whether a dog bites, and virulence as how hard it bites. Two strains of the same species are both pathogenic, but one can be far more virulent than the other.

The traits that raise virulence are called virulence factors: adhesins, capsules, enzymes, and toxins. You will meet all of them in the infection model below.

15.2 · Numbers of invading microbes

ID50 and LD50

ID50 · infectious dose

The number of microbes needed to infect 50% of a sample population. It measures the virulence of a microbe.

Lower ID50 = fewer cells needed = more virulent.

LD50 · lethal dose

The amount of a toxin that kills 50% of a sample population. It measures the potency of a toxin.

Lower LD50 = less toxin needed = more potent.

16.3 · Interactive model

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and PAMPs

PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns) are molecules found on microbes but not on your own cells: LPS, flagellin, peptidoglycan, bacterial DNA, and viral DNA or RNA.

TLRs are receptors on phagocytes and dendritic cells. Each kind of TLR recognizes one kind of pattern. When a TLR binds its PAMP, the cell releases cytokines that call in more phagocytes and switch on T cells and B cells.

A TLR recognizes a whole category of microbe (every gram-negative bacterium has LPS), not one specific species. That is why it is part of innate immunity.

16.5 · Brief overview

Inflammation and fever

Inflammation is a local response to tissue damage. It destroys or walls off the cause and then repairs the tissue. You saw it step by step in the model in 16.2.

Signs of inflammation: PRISH PainRednessImmobilitySwellingHeat
Who is involvedWhat they do
Damaged tissue cellsRelease chemicals that dilate blood vessels and make them leaky.
MacrophagesAlready waiting in the tissue. Their TLRs detect the microbe, and they release cytokines that sound the alarm. Later they clean up.
NeutrophilsTravel in the blood, stick to the vessel wall (margination), squeeze out, and follow chemokines to the site within about an hour. They phagocytize microbes.
Blood vesselsVasodilation causes redness and heat. Increased permeability lets fluid leak out, causing swelling.

Fever: the body's thermostat

Cytokines tell the hypothalamus to release prostaglandins, which reset the body's set point higher. Move the slider and watch body temperature chase the new set point.

Why fever helps

  • Phagocytes and T cells work better at a slightly higher temperature.
  • Boosts interferons and other antimicrobial substances.
  • May slow pathogen growth.
  • Speeds up repair.

When fever hurts

  • Fast heart rate (tachycardia)
  • Acidosis and dehydration
  • Seizures and coma at very high temperatures

Adaptive Immunity: Specific Defenses of the Host

Defenses that learn one specific pathogen, tell self from nonself, and remember.

17.2 · Interactive model

Humoral vs. cellular immunity

Humoral immunity · B cells

  • Mature in red bone marrow
  • Make antibodies
  • Fight pathogens outside cells: in blood and body fluids
  • Extracellular bacteria, toxins, viruses before they enter a cell

Cellular immunity · T cells

  • Mature in the thymus
  • T cell receptors read antigen; T cells release cytokines or kill
  • Fight pathogens inside cells
  • Virus-infected cells, intracellular bacteria

17.3 · Interactive model

Antigens and antibodies

Antigen

A substance that causes the production of antibodies: capsules, cell walls, flagella, fimbriae, toxins, viral capsids and spikes, and also pollen or egg white.

Epitope

The specific small region on an antigen that an antibody binds. One antigen usually has many different epitopes.

Antibody

A Y-shaped protein (immunoglobulin, Ig) that binds one specific epitope and tags it for destruction.

17.5 · The general idea

B cell activation

You have millions of B cells, and each one carries receptors for one epitope. When an antigen arrives, only the B cell whose receptor fits it is chosen (clonal selection). That cell then copies itself (clonal expansion) into antibody-producing plasma cells and long-lived memory B cells.

Type of antigen
17.6 · Antigen presentation

MHC: the cell's display case

MHC class I

Found on all nucleated cells. It displays small pieces of the proteins being made inside the cell. This marks the cell as "self" and lets cytotoxic T cells check what is going on inside.

MHC class II

Found only on antigen-presenting cells (B cells, macrophages, dendritic cells). Displays pieces of microbes they swallowed, and presents them to helper T cells.

17.7 · The general idea

T cells and NK cells

Help

Helper T cells coordinate the response. They release cytokines that activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells, and macrophages.

Kill

Cytotoxic T cells recognize a foreign peptide on MHC I and directly kill that infected cell.

Control

Regulatory T cells calm the response down so it does not overreact or attack self.

Remember

Memory T cells stay after the infection and respond faster next time.

Natural killer (NK) cells

NK cells are not activated by a specific antigen. They kill cells that have lost MHC I or have too little of it, which is a trick many virus-infected and tumor cells use to hide from cytotoxic T cells. They are important early, before T cells are ready.

You are an NK cell. Check each cell's MHC I and decide: spare it or destroy it.
